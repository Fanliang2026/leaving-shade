A Reader's Guide

The World Behind the Story

This section provides background and cultural notes that may help readers understand the people, places, customs, and experiences woven into the memoir.

1. Traditional Opera Theaters and the Apprenticeship System

（戏园子和学徒制度）

A 戏园子 (xìyuánzi) was a traditional Chinese opera theater where regional operas, including Peking Opera, as well as other live performances were staged. Unlike a Western opera house, a xìyuánzi was often a lively social venue where people gathered not only to watch performances, but also to drink tea and socialize.

Traditional opera theaters also played an important role in training performers. Young children who wished to become opera performers often entered an apprenticeship system, studying under an experienced master. Apprentices typically began training at a young age and underwent intensive instruction in singing, acting, movement, martial arts, and stage performance. In exchange for training and living arrangements, they were expected to assist their master or opera troupe with various tasks.

This system played an important role in passing traditional opera skills and performance traditions from one generation to the next.

For Yuzhen, the apprenticeship system became part of her life under very different circumstances. As a young girl, she believed she was being given an opportunity to enter the world of opera and learn from experienced performers. She believed she was becoming a student. In reality, she had been deceived.

She had not been taken in as a student; she had become an unpaid servant.

2. Traditional Chinese Opera

（中国传统戏曲）

Throughout her life, Yuzhen often quoted characters and stories from Chinese opera. For her generation, opera was more than entertainment. It was a way of understanding loyalty, justice, love, family, sacrifice, humor, and infidelity.

The stories became part of everyday conversation, much as people in other cultures quote the Bible, Shakespeare, or well-known films.

3. The Opera Characters in Yuzhen's Everyday Language

Chen Shimei (陈世美)

A famous villain in Chinese opera who abandoned his wife after achieving success and attempted to have her killed to conceal his past. His name has become synonymous with betrayal and infidelity.

Dou E (窦娥)

A tragic heroine from the Yuan dynasty play The Injustice to Dou E. Wrongfully convicted and executed, she became an enduring Chinese symbol of innocence suffering injustice.

According to the legend, before her execution, she declared that her innocence would be proven by three miracles: her blood would not touch the ground, snow would fall in midsummer, and her region would suffer years of drought. All three came true.

Hua Mulan (花木兰)

Hua Mulan is a legendary Chinese heroine known for her courage, filial devotion, and determination. According to the traditional ballad The Ballad of Mulan, she disguises herself as a man and takes her elderly father's place in the army. After years of courageous service, she returns home and reveals her true identity.

Hua Mulan has become a symbol of loyalty, bravery, and devotion to family in Chinese culture.

Outside China, Mulan is widely known through Disney's animated 1998 and live-action 2020 film adaptations, which are based on—but differ in many details from—the traditional legend.

Judge Bao (包拯 / Bao Zheng)

A historical Song dynasty official whose honesty and integrity made him legendary. In Chinese opera and folklore, Judge Bao is portrayed as the ideal magistrate who fearlessly punishes corruption and protects ordinary people, regardless of a person's rank or influence.

Mu Guiying (穆桂英)

Mu Guiying is a legendary heroine in Chinese folklore, celebrated for her intelligence, courage, and military skill. She is best known from the stories of the Yang Family Generals, where she becomes a prominent commander after marrying Yang Zongbao.

Leading troops into battle and defending her country with exceptional strategy and bravery, Mu Guiying has become a lasting symbol of patriotism, leadership, and the strength of women in Chinese culture.

The Oil Seller and the Flower Queen (卖油郎独占花魁)

The story of a humble and honest oil seller who wins the heart of the city's most celebrated courtesan through his sincerity, kindness, and integrity. Unlike her wealthy admirers, he treats her with genuine respect, leading her to choose love and virtue over wealth and social status.

Wang Baochuan and Xue Pinggui (王宝钏与薛仁贵)

A classic Chinese folk romance about loyalty, perseverance, and love. Wang Baochuan, the daughter of a wealthy prime minister, defies her family to marry the poor Xue Pinggui.

After Xue leaves to serve in the military, Wang remains faithful to him for eighteen years, enduring poverty and hardship while waiting for his return. Their eventual reunion symbolizes steadfast love and the triumph of virtue over adversity.

Xu Xian and the White Snake (许仙与白蛇)

Xu Xian and the White Snake is a famous Chinese love story about Xu Xian, a kind-hearted scholar, and Bai Suzhen, a white snake spirit who transforms into a beautiful woman. Despite their different origins, they fall deeply in love and marry.

Their relationship is challenged by the monk Fahai, who believes humans and spirits should not be together. The story celebrates love, devotion, and sacrifice, and has become one of the most beloved legends in Chinese culture.

4. Dagu (大姑)

Literally, “eldest paternal aunt.”

It refers to the oldest sister of one's father. In Chinese, relatives are addressed with specific kinship terms that indicate the exact family relationship and, often, seniority.

5. Dayi (大姨)

Literally, “eldest maternal aunt.”

It refers to the oldest sister of one's mother. Like many Chinese kinship terms, it identifies both the side of the family—maternal or paternal—and the relative's birth order.

6. Shaomai (烧卖)

Shaomai is a traditional Chinese open-topped steamed dumpling, typically filled with pork, shrimp, mushrooms, or sticky rice. It is a popular dish in dim sum and regional Chinese cuisines.

In this memoir, however, the filling is made with chopped lamb and green onion, reflecting a well-known local style of cuisine.

7. The 1970s Department Store Clerk (70年代的售货员)

In the planned economy of 1970s China, working as a sales clerk could be a highly desirable position. A sales clerk enjoyed the security associated with state- or collectively owned employment and, perhaps more importantly, worked at an important point in the distribution of scarce consumer goods.

During this period, many everyday necessities were in limited supply and were distributed through planned channels, sometimes with ration coupons. A sales clerk therefore had a position that could provide not only a stable job—what was often called an “iron rice bowl”—but also access to goods that were difficult for ordinary consumers to obtain.

The occupation could also carry a certain amount of practical influence. Customers depended on shops and distribution organizations for scarce goods, so sales clerks could have considerable social connections and influence. A popular saying of the period even grouped the doctor with a stethoscope, the driver with a steering wheel, the personnel officer, and the sales clerk among coveted occupations.

For this reason, a 1970s Chinese 售货员 (sales clerk) was not quite the equivalent of a modern retail employee. The work took place within a planned distribution system, where the availability and allocation of consumer goods were closely connected to the broader economic system.

This is why Yuzhen's effort to help her younger sister move from a printing factory to a department-store position was more significant than it might appear to a reader today.

8. Food Service Cooperative (食品供销合作社)

The Food Service Cooperative was part of China's broader Supply and Marketing Cooperative (供销合作社) system, which was established and expanded nationwide beginning in the 1950s.

During the 1950s through the 1970s, these cooperatives played an important role in the distribution of food, agricultural products, and everyday necessities within China's planned economy. A local food service cooperative could operate shops, restaurants, and other food-related businesses serving the surrounding community.

Despite their close relationship with the local government, cooperatives were not simply government offices. Depending on the period and locality, they could operate under collective ownership, with their own employees, revenues, and expenses.

With China's economic reforms beginning in the 1980s, the traditional role of many local cooperatives gradually changed. By the 1990s, some smaller cooperatives were restructured, merged, leased, sold, or converted into other forms of commercial organization. As a result, employees of these organizations could have employment and retirement arrangements quite different from those of government employees or workers in large state-owned enterprises.

This background helps explain an important part of Yuzhen's later life: although she had spent many years working successfully within the cooperative system, her employment history did not necessarily provide the same retirement security associated with a government position or a large state-owned enterprise.

9. The Teachers' Residential Compound (教师家属院)

The teachers' residential compounds were housing communities provided by schools or local education authorities for teachers and their families. They were part of China's 单位住房 (work-unit housing) system, which was particularly common from the 1950s through the 1980s and 1990s.

The housing was generally connected to a teacher's workplace rather than being privately purchased. Teachers were often allocated an apartment or house according to factors such as their employment status, seniority, family circumstances, and the availability of housing.

These compounds could include teachers' homes as well as shared facilities such as kitchens, courtyards, laundry areas, and other communal spaces. As a result, they were more than places to live. They often became close-knit communities in which colleagues, neighbors, and their families shared much of their daily lives.

From the 1980s onward, China gradually reformed its housing system. Many work-unit residences were eventually sold to their occupants or transferred into privately owned housing. As a result, many former 教师家属院 (teachers' family residential compounds) changed from institutional housing communities into ordinary residential neighborhoods, although some of the original compounds and buildings still remain.
